Page 1: Factor models characterize the joint behavior of large sets of financial assets through
Page 1: a smaller number of underlying drivers. We develop a network-based framework in
Page 1: which factors emerge naturally from the structure of interactions among assets rather
Page 1: than being imposed statistically. The market is modeled as a system of coupled
Page 1: iterated maps, where assetsΓÇÖ return depends on its own past returns and those of
Page 1: related assets. Effectively modeling the influence of irrational traders whose decisions
Page 1: are based on the past movements of a collection of stocks. The interaction structure
Page 1: between stock returns is defined by a coupling matrix derived from an orthogonal
Page 1: transformation of a Laplacian matrix that gradually links initially isolated clusters
Page 1: into a fully connected network. Within this structure, stable patterns of co-movement
Page 1: arise and can be interpreted as financial factors. The relationship between the initial
Page 1: clustering and the number of observed factors is consistent with a center manifold
Page 1: reduction. We identify an optimal regime in which assetsΓÇÖ variance is effectively
Page 1: explained by the set of factors produced by the network. Our framework offers a
Page 1: structural perspective based on interaction-based factor formation and dimension
Page 1: reduction in financial markets.
Page 1: Keywords: Factor models; Network dynamics; Chaotic systems; Dimensionality reduction; Financial markets

Page 3: 
Page 3: Here we ponder on why factors emerge in the first place? According to the efficient market
Page 3: hypothesis (EMH) stock prices depend only on the fundamentals behind a firm. In EMH the
Page 3: stock prices are only driven by random exogenous signals defined by the economy1 . Still,
Page 3: deviations from fundamental values are observed in trading markets which are driven by
Page 3: investors exhibiting herding behavior or by the use of investment strategies like hedging 12 .
Page 3: These instances creates feedback mechanisms between investors and the market that are well
Page 3: described by dynamical systems. Several works have explored the effect of different feedback
Page 3: mechanisms such as trend following13 , adaptation of expectations14,15 and leverage16 . This
Page 3: perspective motivates a shift: rather than treating factors as externally imposed or purely
Page 3: statistical artifacts, we treat and understand them as emergent properties of a dynamical
Page 3: system.
Page 3: 
Page 3: Modern financial markets are densely interconnected via shared information channels,
Page 3: overlapping investor bases, sectoral relationships, and common economic exposures. If assets
Page 3: influence one another over time, coherent return patterns (i.e. factors) can arise naturally
Page 3: from these interactions. Matrices can be used to define the structure of the interconnections
Page 3: between stocks. Previous works suggest the existence of self-organization in finance17,18 , as
Page 3: transitions to a highly coordinated states were observed in both the dot-com and housing
Page 3: crisis. These are instances of dimensionality reduction that can be achieved by two different connectivity structures. In one case the structure is described by a low-rank matrix19
Page 3: where the rank equals the reduced dimension20 . The alternative case is a center manifold
Page 3: reduction21 where the matrix nullity equals to the reduced dimension. The specific nonlinearities of a system determines if the rank or the nullity of a matrix determines the reduced
Page 3: dimension20,21 . We propose a network-based model that formalizes this structure and offers
Page 3: insight into why certain factors appear from the perspective of dimensionality reduction.

Page 15: DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Page 15: 
Page 15: We explored the possibility that statistical factors emerge from a diffusive process among
Page 15: interacting assets. Within this dynamic structure, stable patterns of co-movement emerge.
Page 15: These patterns serve as latent dimensions of the system, analogous to factors in standard
Page 15: financial models. Crucially, the number and structure of these latent dimensions are determined by the underlying network connectivity rather than by statistical selection procedures.
Page 15: Our numerical findings highlight several key results. First, for M > 2 the number of
Page 15: factors that emerge in the critical region (0.36 < ╬╡ Γëñ 0.58) coincides with the number of
Page 15: initial clusters, or equivalently, with the nullity of the coupling matrix C. This suggests
Page 15: that the emergence of factors is governed more strongly by a center-manifold reduction than
Page 15: by the low-rank hypothesis. Second, the factor loadings across assets are well balanced: no
Page 15: asset is dominated by a single factor. Instead, each asset is influenced by multiple drivers,
Page 15: consistent with the diverse risks present in real markets. Finally, we identify an optimal
Page 15: coupling strength ╬╡ at which the factors generated by the system explain the total variance
Page 15: of each asset in a coherent and interpretable manner.
Page 15: From a Bayesian perspective, the relationship given by Eq.(1) denotes a direct and causal
Page 15: link between factors ft and returns rt . A large body of research seeks to infer Bayesian
Page 15: network connectivity among assets32ΓÇô37 . Recent work emphasizes the need to understand
Page 15: the Bayesian causal network between factors ft themselves in order to reduce biases in
Page 15: factor analysis and improve buy-sell decisions38,39 . However, Bayesian networks permit only
Page 15: one-way casual connections between elements and prohibit feedback loops. In contrast, our
Page 15: model admits feedback loops through the coupling matrix C, thereby relaxing this restriction
Page 15: and offering a more general representation of causal networks.
Page 15: In addition, the dynamical structure of our model makes it well suited for frequency-domain
Page 15: 15
Page 15: 
Page 15: 
Page 16: network applications, where connectivity is studied across different temporal scales40ΓÇô42 . Because our factors emerge from an iterative and interconnected process, the co-movement
Page 16: patterns they generate can be interpreted as dominant spectral modes of the system43 .
Page 16: This makes our model compatible with modern techniques that analyze financial contagion,
Page 16: synchronization, and cyclical dependence in the frequency domain41,44ΓÇô46 . As a result, the
Page 16: framework can be used not only for causal-network inference and factor modeling, but also
Page 16: for applications such as detecting regime shifts, analyzing time-scaleΓÇôspecific spillovers, and
Page 16: identifying structural channels of influence in financial markets40,41,47,48 .
Page 16: Taken together, the presence of feedback loops, endogenous factor formation, and spectral
Page 16: interpretability make our model a general and versatile tool that can support many of the
Page 16: most current and popular approaches for studying network structure in finance.
