Page 1: We investigate the impossibility of universally winning trading strategiesΓÇöthose generating strict
Page 1: profit across all market trajectoriesΓÇöthrough three distinct mathematical paradigms. Fundamentally, under standard admissibility constraints, the existence of such a strategy is a strict subset
Page 1: of strong arbitrage, which is mathematically precluded in competitive markets admitting an equivalent martingale measure. Beyond this rigorous measure-theoretic foundation, we explore analogous limitations in two alternative modeling regimes. Combinatorially, the No-Free-Lunch theorem
Page 1: demonstrates that outperformance requires exploitation of non-uniform market structure, as uniform
Page 1: averaging precludes universal dominance. Computationally, a Turing diagonalization argument constructs an adversarial environment that defeats any computable trading algorithm, shifting the
Page 1: impossibility from exogenous price paths to adaptive adversaries. These mathematical limits are
Page 1: framed by a time-reversal heuristic that establishes a formal analogy between financial martingale
Page 1: measures and thermodynamic detailed balance, resolving the MaxwellΓÇÖs Demon analogy for markets
Page 1: without relying on physically irrelevant Landauer erasure costs. Using the Wheel Options Strategy
Page 1: as a case study, we demonstrate that strategies succeeding ΓÇ£for all practical purposesΓÇ¥ (FAPP) inherently depend on transient regime assumptions, meaning their automated execution systematically
Page 1: amplifies tail risks.

Page 1: I.
Page 1:
Page 1: INTRODUCTION
Page 1:
Page 1: The question of whether a trading strategy can always make moneyΓÇöregardless of what the market doesΓÇö
Page 1: is older than mathematical finance itself. It animated
Page 1: BachelierΓÇÖs 1900 thesis [1], was formalized by Samuelson [2] and Fama [3] into the Efficient Market Hypothesis
Page 1: (EMH), and underlies every retail advertisement promising ΓÇ£passive income in any market condition.ΓÇ¥ The EMH
Page 1: gives the standard economic answer: competitive pressure eliminates consistent excess returns. But the EMH
Page 1: is a statement about equilibrium outcomes across many
Page 1: agents; it is not, by itself, a mathematical proof that no
Page 1: individual strategy can ever win unconditionally.
Page 1: Defining a ΓÇ£universal strategyΓÇ¥ as one that yields a
Page 1: strictly positive terminal wealth across all valid market
Page 1: trajectories is an exceptionally strong condition. Consequently, the intellectual burden lies not merely in proving
Page 1: impossibility, but in precisely delineating the mathematical frameworks under which this impossibility manifests.
Page 1: This paper articulates this impossibility through three
Page 1: distinct theoretical paradigms, carefully distinguishing
Page 1: their incompatible underlying assumptions:
Page 1: 1. Financial (Measure-Theoretic, Sec. III). The
Page 1: rigorous core of the impossibility. We show that under standard admissibility constraints, a universal
Page 1: strategy trivially constitutes an arbitrage opportunity. By the First Fundamental Theorem of Asset
Page 1: Pricing [4, 5], this is precluded in markets admitting an equivalent martingale measure.
Page 1:
Page 1: Γêù karl.svozil@tuwien.ac.at; http://tph.tuwien.ac.at/╦£svozil
Page 1:
Page 1: 2. Combinatorial (Uniform Search, Sec. V). The
Page 1: WolpertΓÇôMacready No-Free-Lunch theorem [6] establishes that under a uniform distribution over all
Page 1: possible discrete evolutions, no strategy uniformly
Page 1: dominates. While markets are not uniform, this
Page 1: boundary highlights that practical trading is an exercise in structure exploitation, not universal algorithmic superiority.
Page 1: 3. Computational (Adversarial, Sec. VI). Shifting the paradigm from exogenous price processes
Page 1: to adaptive environments, we utilize the core diagonalization logic of the Halting Problem [7] to show
Page 1: that no computable strategy can survive an adversary capable of simulating it.
Page 1: We frame these findings with a time-reversal criterion
Page 1: (Sec. II) that motivates a formal analogy with MaxwellΓÇÖs
Page 1: Demon (Sec. IV). We treat this analogy carefully, noting that while the equivalent martingale measure mirrors
Page 1: thermodynamic detailed balance, appealing to physical
Page 1: Landauer erasure costs to explain financial dissipation is
Page 1: quantitatively irrelevant. The Wheel Options Strategy
Page 1: is examined in Sec. VIII as a concrete illustration, and
Page 1: the fragile scope of strategies operating for all practical
Page 1: purposes (FAPP) is discussed in Sec. IX.

Page 2: THE NO-ARBITRAGE FOUNDATION
Page 2: A.
Page 2:
Page 2: B.
Page 2:
Page 2: The Martingale Constraint
Page 2:
Page 2: By the DelbaenΓÇôSchachermayer generalization of the
Page 2: First Fundamental Theorem [4, 5], a market satisfies
Page 2: the condition of No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk
Page 2: (NFLVR) if and only if there exists an equivalent local
Page 2: martingale measure (EMM) Q Γëê P.
Page 2: Corollary 2 (No Universal Strategy). If an equivalent
Page 2: martingale measure Q exists, no universally successful
Page 2: admissible strategy can exist.
Page 2: This is the definitive mathematical constraint. Universality is simply an impossibly strong formulation of
Page 2: arbitrage; its preclusion is the foundational premise of
Page 2: modern asset pricing. Thus, the non-existence of universal strategies is not a deep extension of asset pricing
Page 2: theory, but rather a direct corollary of its foundational
Page 2: no-arbitrage axiom.

Page 2: HONEST BOUNDARIES
Page 2:
Page 2: Admissibility and Arbitrage
Page 2: A.
Page 2:
Page 2: The defining proof of impossibility stems from the First
Page 2: Fundamental Theorem. We must first impose standard
Page 2: regularity. Without admissibility constraints, pathwise
Page 2: gains can be trivially manufactured via doubling strategies (gamblerΓÇÖs ruin constructions), which are financially
Page 2: meaningless due to infinite intermediate drawdown limits.
Page 2: Let the market be defined on a filtered probability
Page 2: space (Γäª, F, {Ft }tΓêê[0,T ] , P). A strategy ╧â is admissible if its wealth process is bounded from below almost
Page 2: surely [5].
Page 2: Theorem 1 (Universality implies Arbitrage). If there
Page 2: exists an admissible, self-financing strategy ╧â with zero
Page 2: initial capital such that ╬á[╧â; P (╧ë)] > 0 for all ╧ë Γêê Γäª,
Page 2: then the market admits a strong arbitrage opportunity.
Page 2: Proof. By assumption, the strategy yields strictly positive terminal wealth pathwise over all ╧ë. Consequently,
Page 2: under the physical measure P, the terminal wealth is
Page 2: strictly positive almost surely (P(╬á > 0) = 1). An admissible, zero-cost, self-financing strategy ending strictly
Page 2: positive almost surely strictly satisfies the condition for
Page 2: a strong arbitrage (arbitrage of the first kind).
Page 2: Remark 2 (Strength Hierarchy). The converse (Arbitrage
Page 2: =ΓçÆ Universal) is generally false. Our definition of universality demands pathwise strict positivity (╬á > 0 for
Page 2: all ╧ë), which is a strictly stronger condition than classical arbitrage (which requires only P(╬á ΓëÑ 0) = 1 and
Page 2: P(╬á > 0) > 0). Because pathwise universality trivially
Page 2: implies strong arbitrage, its impossibility is mathematically immediate once admissibility constraints are imposed under an equivalent martingale measure.

Page 3: What the Analogy Does Not Establish
Page 3:
Page 3: We must aggressively limit the scope of this analogy.
Page 3: It is a formal mapping, not a category-theoretic isomorphism.
Page 3: Transaction costs are not the core mechanism.
Page 3: While transaction costs mimic thermodynamic dissipation, a frictionless market with zero spread still mathematically precludes universal strategies via Corollary 2.
Page 3: LandauerΓÇÖs limit is irrelevant. The minimum energy to erase one bit at room temperature is E Γëê
Page 3: 2.87 ├ù 10ΓêÆ21 J. The Landauer cost of an algorithmic
Page 3: trade is roughly 10ΓêÆ27 USD. Presenting thermodynamic
Page 3: erasure as a financially relevant constraint on algorithms
Page 3: is numerically dishonest.
Page 3: The analogy illuminates why attempts to defeat efficient markets resemble attempts to build perpetual motion machines, but the independent proof of financial impossibility lies strictly in measure theory (Sec. III).
Page 3: V.
Page 3:
Page 3: THE COMBINATORIAL PERSPECTIVE
Page 3: (NFL)
Page 3:
Page 3: The No-Free-Lunch (NFL) theorem of Wolpert and
Page 3: Macready [6] provides a second paradigm. NFL explores
Page 3: optimization over function spaces.
Page 3: Theorem 3 (NFL, WolpertΓÇôMacready). For any two
Page 3: search algorithms A1 and A2 , when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible objective functions, they
Page 3: perform identically.
Page 3: If we map trading to sequential decision-making over
Page 3: discrete price paths, NFL dictates that, averaged uniformly over the set of all possible market evolutions, no
Page 3: strategy can beat the trivial zero-strategy.
Page 3: Model Incompatibility: We explicitly note that
Page 3: NFL is not a direct proof of market efficiency. Financial markets are not drawn from a uniform distribution;
Page 3: they are continuous, highly structured, and governed by
Page 3: no-arbitrage constraints. Furthermore, trading involves
Page 3: sequential risk under a stochastic process, which is categorically different from offline function optimization.
Page 3: However, NFL provides a vital philosophical boundary: it proves that any trading strategy that works must
Page 3: derive its edge purely from exploiting the non-uniform
Page 3: structure of the specific physical measure P. A strategy
Page 3: cannot be inherently, universally superior; it can only be
Page 3: perfectly fitted to a specific, potentially transient, market
Page 3: regime.

Page 3: VII. ANALOGY TO SOCIAL CHOICE:
Page 3: ARROWΓÇÖS IMPOSSIBILITY THEOREM AND
Page 3: FIXED POINTS
Page 3:
Page 3: To contextualize the severity of this computational
Page 3: boundary, it is instructive to draw a parallel to social
Page 3: choice theoryΓÇöspecifically, ArrowΓÇÖs Impossibility Theorem [13]. Just as the Halting Problem establishes a definitive boundary for computer science by proving that no
Page 3: machine can perfectly evaluate every possible input, ArrowΓÇÖs Theorem establishes a definitive boundary for political science by proving that no rank-order voting system
Page 3: can universally aggregate individual preferences into a
Page 3: consistent group decision without violating basic fairness
Page 3: criteria.
Page 3: There is a profound structural parallel between ArrowΓÇÖs Theorem and our adversarial diagonalization argument:
Page 3:
Page 3:
Page 4: 4
Page 4: The ΓÇ£UniversalΓÇ¥ Claim: Arrow investigates a voting
Page 4: rule that is expected to work flawlessly across all
Page 4: possible preference profiles. We investigate a trading strategy that claims to yield strict profit across
Page 4: all possible market paths.
Page 4: The Conflict: Arrow highlights the inherent tension between individual logic and group consistency. Our
Page 4: model highlights the tension between a fixed algorithmic logic and a reactive market environment.
Page 4: The Failure Mode: In social choice, the system breaks
Page 4: down via the Condorcet Paradox and the lack
Page 4: of transitivityΓÇöa cyclical loop where the system
Page 4: cannot decisively rank outcomes. In our computational framework, the breakdown occurs via
Page 4: diagonalizationΓÇöan adversarially constructed path
Page 4: where the strategy is mechanically forced into a losing position.
Page 4: The ΓÇ£WinnerΓÇ¥: Arrow proves that the only universally
Page 4: stable rule collapses to a Dictator. Correspondingly,
Page 4: Theorem 4 demonstrates that the only guaranteed
Page 4: victor is the Adversary, who simulates and systematically defeats the computable strategy.
Page 4: At a deeper mathematical level, as demonstrated
Page 4: by Yanofsky [11], all such diagonal and self-referential
Page 4: paradoxesΓÇöincluding RussellΓÇÖs paradox, Go╠êdelΓÇÖs incompleteness, and TuringΓÇÖs Halting ProblemΓÇöshare a universal structure rooted in the nonexistence of fixed points.
Page 4: In our adversarial market construction, the market acts
Page 4: as a ΓÇ£negationΓÇ¥ operator on the strategyΓÇÖs predictions
Page 4: (moving the price down when the strategy buys, and up
Page 4: when it sells). Because this adversarial response function inherently lacks a fixed point, a universal mapping
Page 4: from trading algorithms to strictly profitable outcomes is
Page 4: mathematically impossible.

Page 4: CASE STUDY: THE WHEEL OPTIONS
Page 4: STRATEGY
Page 4:
Page 4: To ground these theoretical limits, we evaluate the
Page 4: Wheel Strategy: selling a cash-secured put to collect premium, and upon assignment, selling a covered call. It
Page 4: is widely marketed as generating universal, directionless
Page 4: income. Our paradigms predict specific failure modes.
Page 4:
Page 4: A.
Page 4:
Page 4: Failure I: Catastrophic Drop (Time Asymmetry)
Page 4:
Page 4: If the underlying stock crashes post-assignment, the
Page 4: covered call loses value, and capital is trapped at a severe
Page 4: loss. This trajectory is the exact time-reversal of a rapid,
Page 4: profitable rally. By our heuristic (Criterion 1), failure on
Page 4: the reversed path confirms the strategy is not universal.
Page 4:
Page 4: B.
Page 4:
Page 4: Failure II: Slow Bleed (Combinatorial Cost)
Page 4:
Page 4: A persistent downtrend yields premiums but suffers linear capital decay. The NFL paradigm reminds us that a
Page 4: strategy structurally specialized to harvest variance premium in sideways markets mathematically must underperform in sustained directional regimes.
Page 4:
Page 4: C.
Page 4:
Page 4: Failure III: Violent Breakout (Capped Upside)
Page 4:
Page 4: A massive rally results in assignment at the call strike,
Page 4: generating nominal profit but massive opportunity cost.
Page 4: The strategyΓÇÖs structurally capped upside ensures its expected value aligns with the martingale constraint (Corollary 2) by forfeiting the tail events that offset the catastrophic drops (Failure I).

Page 4: IX.
Page 4:
Page 4: FAPP STRATEGIES AND THEIR HONEST
Page 4: SCOPE
Page 4:
Page 4: While true universality is mathematically precluded,
Page 4: strategies can be profitable for all practical purposes
Page 4: (FAPP, following Bell [14]) relative to a specific physical
Page 4: measure P.
Page 4: Market-making earns the spread under normal volatility, and Kelly criterion investing maximizes logarithmic
Page 4: growth under known distributions. However, their success is strictly conditional on the stability of the underlying measure.
Page 4: LoΓÇÖs Adaptive Markets Hypothesis [15] perfectly
Page 4: bridges the gap between our adversarial diagonalization
Page 4: and real markets. As capital floods into a successful
Page 4: FAPP strategy, the market ecosystem adapts. The market transitions from a passive generator (Sec. III) toward
Page 4: the adversarial generator (Sec. VI), eroding the strategyΓÇÖs
Page 4: edge and dynamically creating the exact trajectories necessary to generate its failure set.
Page 4: Crucially, the automated execution of algorithmic
Page 4: FAPP strategies transforms individual theoretical limits into systemic risks. Because failure sets are algorithmically unidentifiable (RiceΓÇÖs Theorem), automated systems will blindly execute into structural breakdownsΓÇö
Page 4: as witnessed during the 1987 portfolio-insurance cascade [16] and the 2010 Flash Crash [17].
