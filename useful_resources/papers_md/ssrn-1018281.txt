Page 2: Abstract
Page 2: Many portfolio strategies are ΓÇ£event-driven,ΓÇ¥ i.e., try to benefit from price
Page 2: movements caused by corporate events such as restructurings, bankruptcies, mergers,
Page 2: acquisitions, or other special situations. Such trading strategies involve payoffs that
Page 2: have discontinuous and skewed distributions that cannot be measured well with
Page 2: conventional risk methods. This paper develops methods to measure the forward-looking
Page 2: risk of portfolios exposed to such discrete events, based on current positions. When
Page 2: events are independent, the portfolio follows a binomial distribution. This approach is
Page 2: extended to the more realistic case where events are not independent. For mergers and
Page 2: acquisitions, empirical estimates of deal break correlations are positive but relatively
Page 2: low, which implies that most event risk is idiosyncratic and diversifiable. This
Page 2: methodology can be used to evaluate the risk and return of different portfolio structures.
Page 2: 
Page 2: JEL Classifications: G11 (portfolio choice), G23 (private financial institutions), G32 (financial risk
Page 2: management)
Page 2: Keywords: merger arbitrage, risk management, hedge funds, value at risk

Page 3: Introduction
Page 3: Many hedge fund strategies are ΓÇ£event-driven,ΓÇ¥ i.e., try to benefit from price movements
Page 3: caused by corporate events such as restructurings, bankruptcies, mergers, acquisitions, or
Page 3: other special situations. Such trading strategies involve payoffs that have discontinuous
Page 3: distributions. Either the event happens or not, which is a binary distribution. These
Page 3: distributions pose a particular challenge to risk management because of their
Page 3: discontinuous nature, as well as the asymmetry of their payoffs. In addition, the history
Page 3: of price movements may not be relevant for measuring risk.
Page 3: Consider, for example, a portfolio manager positioned to take advantage of a
Page 3: proposed merger and acquisition (M&A). The acquirer makes an offer that exceeds the
Page 3: current market price, by an amount known as the ΓÇ£merger premium.ΓÇ¥ Offers can take the
Page 3: form of cash or stock of the bidding company. Right after the deal announcement, the
Page 3: target companyΓÇÖs price jumps up, in proportion to this premium. There is still
Page 3: uncertainty, however, as to the success of the deal, which explains why the price of the
Page 3: target company generally trades at a discount to the offer price, known as the ΓÇ£arbitrage
Page 3: spread.ΓÇ¥ The typical position for risk arbitrageurs is to buy the target company and, in
Page 3: the case of stock deals, short the acquirer. If a deal succeeds, the position generally
Page 3: creates a modest gain.1 In contrast, in the case of deal failure, the target companyΓÇÖs price
Page 3: can fall back to its pre-announcement level, which is a big loss.2 Thus the position is
Page 3: exposed to an asymmetric ΓÇ£deal risk.ΓÇ¥ Such risk, however, cannot be measured from

Page 4: Current industry methods for managing the risk of such positions are rather
Page 4: elementary. Moore et al. (2006) describe a recent survey of risk management methods
Page 4: used by 21 risk arbitrageurs in mergers and acquisitions. The primary method used to
Page 4: control event risk is position limits. The first type is a limit on the fraction of the
Page 4: portfolio invested in any deal, which is typically 10% of the portfolio value. This implies
Page 4: a minimum of 10 positions. In the survey sample, the number of deals varies between 20
Page 4: and 100, with a median of 30. The second type is a limit on the maximum loss of any
Page 4: position, e.g. 5% of the portfolio value if the deal fails. This loss represents a fall in the
Page 4: stock price back to its pre-announcement price. These two types of constraints are
Page 4: largely overlapping.
Page 4: Such single name limits help to diversify the portfolio but are totally ad hoc. This
Page 4: is because, as revealed in the Moore survey, M&A arbitrageurs do not measure their total
Page 4: portfolio risk. Portfolio risk can be summarized, however, with a single measure of
Page 4: downside risk such as Value at Risk (VAR).3

Page 4: traditional risk models, which do not take into account the uncertainty generated by the
Page 4: currently unfolding events. Indeed, Schachter (2006) states that VAR ΓÇ£is not well suitedΓÇ¥

Page 6: I. Drawbacks of Conventional Risk Measures
Page 6: Typical risk measures are returns-based. One example is the volatility of
Page 6: historical returns for the fund over a recent window. This approach is simple but has
Page 6: severe shortcomings. It is unable to react quickly to changes in the portfolio composition
Page 6: or in the trading strategy. In addition, it makes it difficult to understand the structural
Page 6: drivers of risk and hence to manage the risk profile of the portfolio.
Page 6: More recently, position-based risk measures such as VAR have become widely
Page 6: used. Conventional VAR measures combine current position information with the recent
Page 6: history of risk factors, assuming that this history can be applied to the future. Current
Page 6: positions are taken as fixed over the risk horizon.
Page 6: This approach could be defined as ΓÇ£conventional position-based.ΓÇ¥ Generally, this
Page 6: is a vast improvement over returns-based methods because it can handle changes in
Page 6: portfolio composition and can be used to manage portfolio risk.
Page 6: In the case of event-driven funds, however, the conventional application of
Page 6: position-based VAR methods can be misleading for a number of reasons. First, the event
Page 6: itself changes the nature of the stochastic process, from a random walk to a discreteoutcome process. Thus, historical data cannot be used in the usual fashion. As an
Page 6: example, consider the data provided by Mitchell and Pulvino (2001). They report that,
Page 6: over the period 1963 to 1998, a value-weighted portfolio of M&A deals had an annual
Page 6: realized volatility of 9.3%. This number represents the actual risk of a portfolio invested
Page 6: in an average of 31 M&A positions taken after the announcement. They do not provide
Page 6: the volatility of the same portfolio before the announcement, but surely this should be
Page 6: less than that of a value-weighted market index because the latter is better diversified.
Page 6:

Page 7: substantial.
Page 7: Another problem is that the recent history includes the announcement of the event
Page 7: itself. This creates a large jump in the price of the target, which artificially increases the
Page 7: risk measure. The shorter is the window, the larger is the effect. As an example, take an
Page 7: unleveraged M&A hedge fund with more than 150 positions. From May 2005 to April
Page 7: 2007, this fund had a return-based volatility of 2.2% pa. Conventional risk forecasts
Page 7: were also measured for this fund due to the availability of position data, applied each

Page 7: position-based risk measures. Such adjustments cannot be universal, however. This is
Page 7: because the relationship between a conventional position-based risk measure and its
Page 7: actual counterpart depends on the structure of the portfolio, which involves the number of
Page 7: positions, the distribution of deal payoffs, as well as historical risk measures. Note, for
Page 7: instance, that the actual risk of the first M&A portfolio was 9.3%, which is much higher
Page 7: than the risk of the second M&A portfolio, which was 2.2%. This is because the first
Page 7: portfolio was much less diversified, with 31 positions against 150 for the second.

Page 14: probability of failure by about 9 percent. This contemporaneous dependency on the

Page 25: V. Implications for Assessing Economic Capital and Leverage
Page 25: This methodology can be used to infer economic capital levels, or conversely the
Page 25: number of deals required to keep a fixed level of risk for event-driven portfolios.
Page 25: Leverage can be assessed from economic capital. Recall that with one deal only,
Page 25: economic capital is $15, implying a maximum leverage of $100/$15.0=6.7 at which point
Page 25: the rate of return on economic capital is $2.0/$15.0=13.3% per quarter.
Page 25: For a 10-deal portfolio with a correlation of 0.03, VAR is $9.0, at a minimum
Page 25: 99.9% confidence level. As a result, the portfolio could be leveraged by a factor of
Page 25: $100/$9.0, or about 10 times the original equity investment, generating an expected
Page 25: return of $2.0/$9.0=22%.
Page 25: This analysis shows that increasing the number of deals substantially lowers risk,
Page 25: because deal risk is largely idiosyncratic. When N increases to 30 and 100, economic
Page 25: capital drops to $5.00 and $3.80, respectively. Figure 5 illustrates how economic capital
Page 25: decreases with the number of deals. Lower risk allows greater leverage, increasing

Page 30: VI. Conclusions
Page 30: Event-driven portfolio strategies face discrete deal risk. In contrast with other
Page 30: types of market risks, where distributions are continuous, deal risk is represented by a
Page 30: binary variable, which is the success or failure of a deal. This type of risk cannot be
Page 30: captured by conventional position-based risk measures.
Page 30: Current positions, however, can be used to construct the distribution of portfolio
Page 30: returns. To do so, the risk manager needs estimates of the probability of success for each
Page 30: deal, of the payoffs from success and failure, and of joint correlations across deals. This
Page 30: paper develops new methods to construct quantitative measures of portfolio risk for
Page 30: event-driven portfolios.
Page 30: Once constructed, this distribution can be summarized by the usual risk measures,
Page 30: such as Value at Risk, which can be used to assess acceptable levels of leverage. This
Page 30: paper finds that, in the case of independent deals, the economic capital required to
Page 30: support a portfolio of 30 independent deals is six times lower than for a single-deal
Page 30: portfolio. Thus, this diversified portfolio could be levered six times more than a singledeal position and still maintain the same level of risk. In the meantime, this higher
Page 30: leverage implies a higher return on capital.
Page 30: In practice, however, these diversification benefits are limited because of
Page 30: clustering across deal breaks. This paper provides empirical estimates indicating that the
Page 30: average deal-break correlation is around 0.03 for large North American deals. This
Page 30: positive correlation decreases the benefits from diversification and increases the required
Page 30: economic capital.

Page 31: This methodology can be used to improve current risk management practices for
Page 31: event-driven portfolios. Apparently, portfolio managers primarily rely on position limits
Page 31: to control risk. Similar methods were used to manage credit risk before the generalized
Page 31: application of modern risk measurement methods.
Page 31: Without such quantitative methods, it seems difficult to assess the optimal riskreturn profile of the portfolio. With limited expertise or resources, increasing the number
Page 31: of deals is costly, because it decreases the average expected return per deal. On the other
Page 31: hand, increasing the number of deals does provide diversification benefits. The optimal
Page 31: portfolio represents the point where the marginal cost equates the marginal benefit from
Page 31: increasing deals. Without a formal measure of the marginal benefit, however, this
Page 31: optimal position cannot be identified.
Page 31: Armed with these portfolio measurement tools, the portfolio manager should
Page 31: search for deals that are profitable, yet not too correlated across each other. This paper
Page 31: provides the means to assess the costs and benefits of different structures for event-driven
Page 31: portfolios.
