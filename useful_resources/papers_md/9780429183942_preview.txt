Page 11: Algorithms have been around since the day trading has started. But they have
Page 11: gained importance with the advent of computers and the automation of trading. Efficiency in execution has taken center stage and with that, speed and instantaneous
Page 11: processing of asset related information have become important. In this book, we will
Page 11: focus on the methodology rooted in financial theory and demonstrate how relevant
Page 11: dataΓÇöboth in the high frequency and in the low frequency spacesΓÇöcan be meaningfully analyzed. The intention is to bring both the academics and the practitioners
Page 11: together. We strive to achieve what George Box (first authorΓÇÖs teacher) once said:
Page 11: ΓÇ£One important idea is that science is a means whereby
Page 11: learning is achieved, not by mere theoretical speculation
Page 11: on the one hand, nor by the undirected accumulation of
Page 11: practical facts on the other, but rather by a motivated
Page 11: iteration between theory and practice.ΓÇ¥
Page 11: We hope that we provide a framework for relevant inquiries on this topic. To quote
Page 11: Judea Pearl,
Page 11: ΓÇ£You cannot answer a question that you cannot ask, and
Page 11: you cannot ask a question that you have no words for.ΓÇ¥
Page 11: The emphasis of this book, the readers will notice, is on data analysis with guidance
Page 11: from appropriate models. As C.R. Rao (first authorΓÇÖs teacher) has aptly observed:
Page 11: All knowledge is, in final analysis, history.
Page 11: All Sciences are, in the abstract, mathematics.
Page 11: All judgements are, in the rationale, statistics.
Page 11: This book gives an inside look into the current world of Electronic Trading and
Page 11: Quantitative Strategies. We address actual challenges by presenting intuitive and
Page 11: innovative ideas on how to approach them in the future. The subject is then augmented through a more formal treatment of the necessary quantitative methods with
Page 11: a targeted review of relevant academic literature. This dual approach is also reflective of the dynamics, typical of quants working on a trading floor where commercial
Page 11: needs, such as time to market, often supersede consideration of rigorous models in
Page 11: favor of intuitively simple approaches.
Page 11: Our unique approach in this book is to provide the reader with hands-on tools.
Page 11: This book will be accompanied by a collection of practical Jupyter Notebooks where
Page 11: select methods are applied to real data. This will allow the readers to go beyond theory

Page 12: Wherever possible the charts and tables in the book can be generated directly from

Page 12: While reading and working through this book, the reader should be able to gain insight
Page 12: into how the field of Electronic Trading and Quantitative Strategies, one of the most
Page 12: active and exciting spaces in the world of finance, has evolved.
Page 12: This book is divided into five parts.
Page 12: ΓÇó Part I sets the stage. We narrate the history and evolution of Equity Trading
Page 12: and delve into a review of the current features of modern Market Structure. This
Page 12: gives the readers context on the business aspects of trading in order for them to
Page 12: understand why things work as they do. The next section will provide a brief highlevel foundational overview of market microstructure which explains and models
Page 12: the dynamics of a trading venue heavily influenced by the core mechanism of
Page 12: how trading takes place: the price-time priority limit-order book with continuous
Page 12: double auction. This will set the stage for the introduction of a critical but elusive
Page 12: concept in trading: Liquidity.
Page 12: ΓÇó Part II provides an overview of discrete time series models applied to equity
Page 12: trading. We will address univariate and multivariate time series models of both
Page 12: mean and variance of asset returns, and other associated quantities such as volume. While somewhat less used today because of high frequency trading, these
Page 12: models are important as conceptual frameworks, and act as baselines for more
Page 12: advanced methods. We also cover some essential concepts in Point Processes as
Page 12: the actual trading data can come at irregular intervals. The last chapter of Part II
Page 12: will present more advanced topics like State-Space Models and modern Machine

Page 19: A Brief History of Stock Trading
Page 19: 
Page 19: Why We Trade: Companies need capital to operate and expand their businesses. To
Page 19: raise capital, they can either borrow money then pay it back over time with interest,
Page 19: or they can sell a stake (equity) in the company to an investor. As part owner of
Page 19: the company, the investor would then receive a portion of the profits in the form of
Page 19: dividends. Equity and Debt, being scarce resources, have additional intrinsic value
Page 19: that change over time; their prices are influenced by factors related to the performance
Page 19: of the company, existing market conditions and in particular, the future outlook of the
Page 19: company, the sector, the demand and supply of capital and the economy as a whole.
Page 19: For instance, if interest rates charged to borrow capital change, this would affect the
Page 19: value of existing debt since its returns would be compared to the returns of similar
Page 19: products/companies that offer higher/lower rates of return. When we discuss trading
Page 19: in this book we refer to the act of buying and selling debt or equity (as well as other
Page 19: types of instruments) of various companies and institutions among investors who have
Page 19: different views of their intrinsic value.
Page 19: These secondary market transactions via trading exchanges also serve the purpose
Page 19: of ΓÇ£price discoveryΓÇ¥ (OΓÇÖHara (2003) [275]). Buyers and sellers meet and agree on a
Page 19: price to exchange a security. When that transaction is made public, it in turn informs
Page 19: other potential buyers and sellers of the most recent market valuation of the security.
Page 19: The evolution of the trading process over the last 200 years makes for an incredible tale of ingenuity, fierce competition and adept technology. To a large extent, it
Page 19: continues to be driven by the positive (and at times not so positive) forces of making
Page 19: profit, creating over time a highly complex, and amazingly efficient mechanism, for
Page 19: evaluating the real value of a company.
Page 19: The Origins of Equity Trading: The tale begins on May 17, 1792 when a group of
Page 19: 24 brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement. This bound the group to trade only
Page 19: with each other under specific rules. This agreement marked the birth of the New
Page 19: York Stock Exchange (NYSE). While the NYSE is not the oldest Stock Exchange in

Page 20: Archipelago and Island ECNs.

Page 29: 1.3.1
Page 29: 
Page 29: How Double Auction Markets Work
Page 29: 
Page 29: The most common approach used by modern electronic exchanges can be termed,
Page 29: as time/price priority, continuous double auction trading system. The term double
Page 29: auction signifies that, unlike a common auction with one auctioneer dealing with
Page 29: potential buyers, in this case there are multiple buyers and multiple sellers participating in the process at the same time. These buyers and sellers interact with the
Page 29: exchange by sending instructions electronically, via a network protocol to a specialized software and hardware infrastructure called: The Matching Engine. It has two
Page 29: main components: The Limit Order Book and the Matching Algorithm.
Page 29: Limit Order Book (LOB): It is a complex data structure that stores all non-executed
Page 29: orders with associated instructions. It is highly specialized so as to be extremely fast
Page 29: to insert/update/delete orders and then able to sort them and to retrieve aggregated
Page 29: information. For an active stock, the LOB can be updated and queried thousands of
Page 29: times every second, so it must be highly efficient and able to handle a high degree
Page 29: of concurrency to ensure that the state is always correct. The LOB is comprised of
Page 29: two copies of the core data structure, one for Buy orders and one for Sell orders often
Page 29: referred to as the two ΓÇ£sidesΓÇ¥ of the order book. This structure is the core abstraction
Page 29: for all electronic exchanges and so it is very important to understand it in detail.
Page 29: The LOB supports three basic instructions: Insert, cancel, and amend, with insert
Page 29: initiating a new order, cancel removing an existing order from the market and amend
Page 29: modifying some of the parameters of the existing order. New orders must specify
Page 29: ΓÇ£order typeΓÇ¥ and associated parameters necessary to fully encapsulate the trader decision. We will review order types in more detail later, but we start with the two main
Page 29: types: The limit order and the market order. The main difference between the two
Page 29: 
Page 29: 
Page 30: 14
Page 30: 
Page 30: Algorithmic Trading and Quantitative Strategies
Page 30: 
Page 30: order types is that a limit order has a price associated to it while a market order does
Page 30: not.
Page 30: Accounting for limit and market orders, there are eight events at any given time,
Page 30: four on either side, that can alter the state of the order book:
Page 30: ΓÇó Limit Order Submission: A limit order is added to the queue at the specified
Page 30: price level.
Page 30: ΓÇó Limit Order Cancellation: An outstanding limit order is expired or canceled
Page 30: and is therefore removed from the Limit Order Book (LOB).
Page 30: ΓÇó Limit Order Amendment: An outstanding limit order is modified by the original
Page 30: sender (such as changing order size).
Page 30: ΓÇó Execution: Buy and sell orders at appropriate prices are paired by the matching
Page 30: algorithm (explained below) into a binding transaction and are removed from the
Page 30: LOB
Page 30: Matching Algorithm: This software component is responsible for interpreting the
Page 30: various events to determine if any buy and sell orders can be matched in an execution. When multiple orders can be paired the algorithm uses the so-called price/time
Page 30: priority meaning that first the order with the most competitive prices are matched and
Page 30: when prices are equal the order that arrived prior is chosen. As we will see in later
Page 30: chapters this is only one of the possible algorithms used in practice but it is by far the
Page 30: most common. We will go into more details in the next sections.
Page 30: The matching algorithm operates continuously throughout the trading hours. In
Page 30: order to ensure an orderly start and end, this continuous session is usually complemented by a couple of discrete auctions. The trading day generally starts with an open
Page 30: auction, then followed by the main continuous session, and ends with a closing auction. Some markets like Japan also have a lunch break which might be preceded by a
Page 30: morning closing auction and followed by an afternoon opening auction. We will now
Page 30: discuss these main market phases in chronological order.

Page 38: Limit orders make up a significant percentage (70%) of stock market trading activity. The main advantage of a limit order is that there is no price risk associated to it,
Page 38: that is, when the order is executed the limit price is the maximum (for a buy order) or
Page 38: minimum (for a sell order) price that will be achieved. But if the limit order is not marketable, the execution is not guaranteed and the time to get an order executed depends
Page 38: on various market factors. The trade-off between limit orders and marketable orders
Page 38: depends on the investorΓÇÖs need for immediate liquidity and the fill probability of limit
Page 38: orders. The limit price chosen (how deep in the order book is the order placed) as well
Page 38: as the amount of liquidity ahead of the submitted order (how many shares will need
Page 38: to trade before the order gets executed following, for instance, a price/time priority
Page 38: order matching of the exchange) affect both the order fill probability and its expected
Page 38: time to fill. These two metrics are of particular relevance for execution algorithms
Page 38: and will be studied in more depth later.
Page 38: The execution of limit orders does affect how the quotes are posted and are
Page 38: updated. If the size of a market order exceeds the number of shares available at the
Page 38: top of book, it is usually split and is executed at consecutive order book levels until
Page 38: the order is filled. Market orders are usually restricted to be filled within a day and
Page 38: orders placed after the markets close might be entered the next day.22

Page 47: While it is possible to store daily data as a single entry per day and per instrument, investors building intraday strategies likely need tick by tick data of all the
Page 47: events occurring in the market place. To help classify these events, exchanges

Page 51: While historically a large swath of modeling for developing strategies was carried
Page 51: out on daily or minute bar data sets, the past fifteen years have seen a significant
Page 51: rise in the usage of raw market data in an attempt to extract as much information
Page 51: as possible, and act on it before the opportunity (or market inefficiency) dissipates.
Page 51: Market data, itself, comes in various levels of granularity (and price!) and can be
Page 51: subscribed to either directly from exchanges (known as ΓÇ£direct feedsΓÇ¥) or from data
Page 51: vendors aggregating and distributing it. The level of detail of the feed subscribed to
Page 51: is generally described as Level I, Level II or Level III market data.
Page 51: Level I Data: Trade and BBO Quotes: Level I market data is the most basic form
Page 51: of tick-by-tick data. Historically, the Level I data feed would only refer to trade information (Price, Size, Time of each trade reported to the tape) but has grown over time
Page 51: into a term generally accepted to mean both trades and top of book quotes. While the
Page 51: trade feed updates with each trade printed on the tape, the quote feed tends to update
Page 51: much more frequently each time liquidity is added to, or removed from, the top of
Page 51: book (a rough estimate in liquid markets is the quotes present more updates than the
Page 51: trades by about an order of magnitude).

Page 52: In order to build strategies, the timing of each event must be as precise as possible. While the time reported for trade and quotes is the matching engine time at the
Page 52: exchange, most databases also store a reception or record time to reflect the potential
Page 52: latency between the trade event and one being aware that it did happen and being able
Page 52: to start making decisions on it. Accounting for this real-world latency is a necessary
Page 52: step for researchers building strategies on raw market data for which opportunities
Page 52: may be very short lived and may be impossible to exploit by participants who are not
Page 52: fast enough.
Page 52: The Level I data is enough to reconstruct the Best Bid and Offer (BBO) of the
Page 52: market. However, in fragmented markets, it is also useful to obtain an aggregated
Page 52: consolidated view of all available liquidity at a given price level across all exchanges.
Page 52: Market data aggregators usually provide this functionality for users who do not wish
Page 52: to reconstruct the full order book themselves.
Page 52: Finally, it is worth noting that even Level I data contains significant additional
Page 52: information in the form of trade status (canceled, reported late, etc.) and, trade and
Page 52: quote qualifiers. These qualifiers provide granular details such as whether a trade was
Page 52: an odd lot, a normal trade, an auction trade, an Intermarket Sweep, an average price
Page 52: reporting, on which exchange it took place, etc. These details can be used to better
Page 52: analyze the sequence of events and decide if a given print should be used to update
Page 52: the last price and total volume traded at that point in time or not. For instance, if not
Page 52: processed appropriately, a trade reported to the tape out of sequence could result in a
Page 52: large jump in price because the market may have since moved, and consequently the
Page 52: trade status is an indication that the print should not be utilized as it is.
Page 52: Capturing raw market data, whether to build a research database or to process
Page 52: it in real-time to make trading decisions, requires significant investments and expert
Page 52: knowledge to handle all the inherent complexity. Hence, one should carefully consider the trade-off between the expected value that can be extracted from the extra
Page 52: granularity, and the additional overhead compared to simpler solutions such as using
Page 52: binned data.
Page 52: Level II Data: Market Depth: Level II market data contains the same information as
Page 52: Level I, but with the addition of quote depth data. The quote feed displays all lit limit
Page 52: order book updates (price changes, addition or removal of shares quoted) at any level
Page 52: in the book, and for all of the lit venues in fragmented markets. Given the volume of
Page 52: data generated and the decreasing actionable value of quote updates as their distance
Page 52: to top of book increases, some users limit themselves to the top five or ten levels of
Page 52: the order book when they collect and/or process the data.

Page 52: dataΓÇöprovides the most granular view of the activity in the limit order book. Each
Page 52: order arriving is attributed a unique ID, which allows for its tracking over time, and
Page 52: is precisely identified when it is executed, canceled or amended. Similar to Level II,
Page 52: the data set contains intraday depth of book activity for all securities in an exchange.

Page 52: ordered by timestamp, it is possible to build a full (with national depth) book at any

Page 58: Binned Data
Page 58: The first natural extension to daily data sets is a discretization of the day into bins
Page 58: ranging from a few seconds to 30 minutes. The features collected are comparable to
Page 58: the ones relevant for daily data sets (period volume, open, high, low, close, VWAP,

Page 58: to market frictions. The major advantage of binned-data is that discrete time series
Page 58: methods can be readily used.

Page 58: neutral long-short baskets, momentum and mean-reversion strategies, etc.). The main
Page 58: benefit they provide is a significant dimension reduction compared to raw market
Page 58: data (390 rows per stock per day in the US compared to millions for raw data for the
Page 58: liquid stocks), which allows researchers to perform rapid and efficient backtesting as
Page 58: they search for alpha. However, increasing data frequency from daily data to intraday
Page 58: minute bars also presents challenges. In particular, relationships that appear to be
Page 58: stable using close-to-close values become much noisier as granularity increases and
Page 58: signals become harder to extract (e.g., drop in correlation between assets, pricing
Page 58: inefficiency moves due to sudden liquidity demand, etc.). Similarly, for less liquid
Page 58: assets with a low trade frequency, there may not be any trading activity for shorter
Page 58: durations, resulting in empty bins.

Page 60: Market Microstructure: Economic Fundamentals of Trading
Page 60: 
Page 60: We first provide a brief review of market microstructure, an area of finance that
Page 60: studies how the supply and demand of liquidity results in actual transactions and modifies the subsequent state of the market, and then delve into some critical operational
Page 60: concepts. We draw upon key review papers by Madhavan (2000) [254], Biais, Glosten
Page 60: and Spatt (2005) [39] and OΓÇÖHara (2015) [276]. The core idea is that the efficient market hypothesis, which postulates that the equity price impounds all the information
Page 60: about the equity, may not hold due to market frictions. Algorithmic trading essentially
Page 60: exploits the speed with which investors acquire the information and how they use it
Page 60: along with the market frictions that arise mainly due to demand-supply imbalances.
Page 60: Taking an informational economics angle, Madhavan (2000) [254] provides a market
Page 60: microstructure analysis framework following three main categories.
Page 60: ΓÇô Price Formation and Price Discovery: How do prices impound information over
Page 60: time and how do the determinants of trading costs vary?
Page 60: ΓÇô Market Design: How do trading rules affect price formation?
Page 60: Market design generally refers to a set of rules that all players have to follow in
Page 60: the trading process. These include the choice of tick size, circuit breakers which
Page 60: can halt trading in the event of large price swings, the degree of anonymity and
Page 60: the transparency of the information to market participants, etc. Markets around
Page 60: the world and across asset classes can differ significantly in these types of rules,
Page 60: creating a diverse set of constraints and opportunity for algorithmic traders. Some
Page 60: early research on the effects of market design lead to following broad conclusions:
Page 60: ΓÇô Centralized trading via one single market tends to result in more efficient price
Page 60: discovery with smaller bid-ask spreads. In the presence of multiple markets, the
Page 60: primary markets (such as NYSE, NASDAQ) remain the main sources of price
Page 60: discovery (see Hasbrouck (1995) [181]).
Page 60: ΓÇô Despite market participantsΓÇÖ preference for continuous, automated limit order
Page 60: book markets, theoretical models suggest that multilateral trading approaches
Page 60: such as single-price call auctions are the most efficient in processing diverse
Page 60: information (See Mendelson (1982) [263] and Ho et al. (1985) [197]).
Page 60: ΓÇô Transparency: How do the quantity, quality and speed of information provided to
Page 60: market participants affect the trading process?
Page 60: Transparency which is broadly classified into pre-trade (lit order book) and posttrade (trade reporting to the public, though at different time lags) is often a trade-off.

Page 64: they involve negotiations between two parties, still mostly happen in a manual
Page 64: fashion or over the phone and then get reported to an appropriate exchange for

Page 64: The second category of market making involves the provision of quasicontinuous, immediately accessible quotes on an electronic venue. With the
Page 64: advent of electronic trading described in the previous section, market makers
Page 64: originally seated on the exchange floors have progressively been replaced by electronic liquidity providers (ELP). The ELP leverage fast technology to disseminate timely quotes across multiple exchanges and develop automated quantitative strategies to manage their inventory and the associated risk. As such, most
Page 64: ELP can be classified as high frequency traders. They derive their profits from
Page 64: three main sources: From liquidity rebates on exchanges that offer maker-taker
Page 64: fee structure, from spread earned when successfully buying on the bid and selling on the offer, and from short-term price moves favorable to their inventory.
Page 64: Hendershott, Brogaard and Riordan (2014) [191] find that HFT activity tends to
Page 64: be concentrated in large liquid stocks and postulate that this can be attributed
Page 64: to a combination of larger profit opportunities emanating from trades happening
Page 64: more often, and from easier risk management due to larger liquidity that allows
Page 64: for easier exit of unfavorable positions at a reasonable cost.
Page 64: 
Page 64: (e) Risk Management: In the existing literature on informed trading, it is observed
Page 64: that liquidity supplying risk-neutral market makers are adversely selected by
Page 64: informed traders suddenly moving prices against them. For example, a market
Page 64: maker buy quote tends to be executed when large sellers are pushing the price
Page 64: down, resulting in even lower prices in the near term. This significant potential
Page 64: asymmetry of information at any point in time emphasizes the need for market
Page 64: makers to employ robust risk management techniques, particularly in the domain
Page 64: of inventory risk. For a market maker, risk management is generally accomplished
Page 64: by first adjusting market quotes upward or downward to increase the arrival rate
Page 64: of sellers or buyers and consequently adjusting the inventory in the desired direction. If biasing the quotes does not result in a successful inventory adjustment,
Page 64: the market makers generally employ limit orders to cross the spread.
Page 64: Ho and Stoll (1981) [196] introduced a market making model in which the market
Page 64: makerΓÇÖs objective is to maximize profit while minimizing the probability of ruin by
Page 64: determining the optimal bid-ask spread to quote. The inventory held evolves through
Page 64: the arrival of bid and ask orders, where the arrival rate is taken to be a function of bid
Page 64: and ask prices. Their model also incorporates the relevant notion of the size dependence of spread on the market makerΓÇÖs time horizon. The longer the remaining time,
Page 64: the greater potential for adverse move risk for liquidity providers, and vice versa.
Page 64: This is consistent with observed spreads. In most markets, the spread is wider at the
Page 64: beginning of the day, narrowing toward the close. An additional reason annotated for
Page 64:

Page 65: the wider spread right after the beginning of the trading day is due to the existence
Page 65: of potentially significant information asymmetry accumulated overnight. As market
Page 65: makers are directly exposed to that information asymmetry, they tend to quote wider
Page 65: spreads while the price discovery process unfolds following the opening of continuous trading, and progressively tighten them as uncertainty about the fair price for the
Page 65: asset dissipates.
Page 65: Understanding the dynamics of market makers inventory and risk management,
Page 65: and their effect on spreads, has direct implications for the practitioners who intend to
Page 65: deploy algorithmic trading strategies as the spread paid to enter and exit positions is a
Page 65: non-negligible source of cost that can erode the profitability of low alpha quantitative
Page 65: strategies.
Page 65: Hendershott and Seasholes (2007) [195] confirms that market makersΓÇÖ inventories
Page 65: are negatively correlated with previous price changes and positively correlated with
Page 65: subsequent changes. This is consistent with market makers first acting as a dampener
Page 65: of buying or selling pressure by bearing the risk of temporarily holding inventory
Page 65: in return for earning the spread and thus potential price appreciation from market
Page 65: reversal. This model easily links liquidity provision and the dynamics of asset prices.
Page 65: Finally, it has been observed that there is a positive correlation of market makers
Page 65: inventory with subsequent prices changes, inventories can complement past returns
Page 65: when predicting future returns. Since inventories are not publicly known, market participants use different proxies to infer their values throughout the day. Two commonly
Page 65: used proxies are trade imbalances (the net excess of buy or sell initiated trade volume)
Page 65: and spreads. Trade imbalance aims at classifying trades, either buy initiated or sell initiated, by comparing their price with the prevailing quote. Given that market makers
Page 65: try to minimize their directional risk, they can only accommodate a limited amount
Page 65: of non-diversified inventory over a finite period of time. As such, spread sizes, and
Page 65: more particularly their sudden variation, have also been used as proxies for detecting
Page 65: excess inventory forcing liquidity providers to adjust their positions.
