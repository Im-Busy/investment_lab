Page 1: Abstract
Page 1: Advanced sentiment analysis systems, which typically produce a positive or negative
Page 1: signal on firms for financial traders, consider financial events when calculating aggregate
Page 1: sentiment scores. However, these systems typically produce overly noisy signals for traders
Page 1: because they aggregate the information culled from the events rather than address each
Page 1: distinct event-type differently (all event-types receive the same weight in a textΓÇÖs final
Page 1: sentiment score). We demonstrate that event-types have different informative values for
Page 1: traders, and specific event-types may be particularly relevant for specific investment
Page 1: periods. In this work we evaluate and compare the effects of event-type signals and
Page 1: sentiment analysis signals, automatically extracted from a given text, on stock prices in
Page 1: different investment periods. We show that traders using trading strategies based on eventtypes and sentiment analysis obtain superior performance compared to acceptable
Page 1: benchmarks.

Page 3:
Page 3: News of financial events is presumed to affect stock prices. There are different opinions in
Page 3: the literature on the extent to which news is reflected in stock prices and the time lag
Page 3: involved before such news is reflected. These questions are fundamentally tied to Eugene
Page 3: FamaΓÇÖs Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) (Fama 1970). Many studies have since
Page 3: attempted to identify methods in which publicly available information can, nevertheless,
Page 3: be used to obtain excess returns (Antweiler and Frank 2004; Davis et al. 2012; Finnerty
Page 3: 1976; Grossman and Stiglitz 1980). These studies argue against FamaΓÇÖs hypothesis that
Page 3: states that it is impossible to beat the market because prices already incorporate and reflect
Page 3: all relevant information.
Page 3: In this work, we analyze which events reported on digital media can be valuable signals
Page 3: for traders in making trading decisions and whether they enhance or diminish the value of
Page 3: sentiment analysis signals. Subsequent questions are whether events should be considered
Page 3: separately or aggregately, and for which periods relevant securities should be held after
Page 3: obtaining such signals. To answer these questions, we used VIP (Visual Information
Page 3: Extraction Platform), an automatic information extraction platform, for the text analysis
Page 3: and the Quantopian Research Platform for the performance analysis. VIP processes,
Page 3: analyzes, and converts unstructured financial data into structured data sets of stock-specific
Page 3: sentiments tied to business events. This tool allows us to identify various sentiments and
Page 3: events that are related to public companies. Using Quantopian, one can create trading
Page 3: algorithms and test them against historical data (a process known as backtesting). We also
Page 3: used Alphalens, a component of the Quantopian Research Platform, which was designed
Page 3: to analyze alpha factors.

Page 4: Related work
Page 4: The question of how to exploit unstructured firm-related digital data has occupied many
Page 4: financial economists and computer scientists. Early work argues that by merely observing
Page 4: the tone of the text about a particular firm on digital media, traders can obtain a valuable
Page 4: signal. For example, Tetlock et al. (2008) found that the occurrence of negative words in
Page 4: news articles about firms could function as a valuable signal for traders. With advances in
Page 4: the field of NLP, researchers have adopted more sophisticated approaches to sentiment
Page 4: analysis and information extraction, and applied them on financial texts (Davis et al. 2012;
Page 4: Heston and Sinha 2015; Li et al. 2014; Schumaker and Chen 2009; Zhang and Skiena
Page 4: 2010). Boudoukh et al. (2016) showed that public firm-level news is a meaningful
Page 4: component of stock return variance. Distinguishing between information that is relevant to
Page 4: the firmΓÇÖs fundamentals (e.g., analyst recommendations, acquisitions, products, etc.) and
Page 4: irrelevant information (e.g. executive changes, dividends, etc.), they argued that only
Page 4: certain events should be considered relevant signals. This level of granularity is, however,
Page 4: inadequate on two counts. First, after distinguishing between events that are likely to
Page 4: impact stock prices and those that are not, these researchers treated all relevant events
Page 4: equally in their analysis. Rather than considering the individual impact of every event type, the researchers looked at the aggregate effect of all relevant events. Second, we argue
Page 4: that events should be further classified to distinguish between subsets of events. For
Page 4: example, product events might involve product trials or product recalls. Each event-type
Page 4: would obviously have a very different impact on stock prices. Boudoukh et al. ( 2016) do
Page 4: not make such distinction. Ding et al. (2014) used a deep neural network model in order to

Page 7: The Quantopian Research Platform
Page 7: The Quantopian Research platform4 is an IPython notebook environment that is used for
Page 7: research and data analysis during algorithm creation. It is also used for analyzing the past
Page 7: performance of algorithms. The IDE (interactive development environment) is used for
Page 7: writing algorithms and kicking off backtests using historical data. We uploaded the signals
Page 7: described in the previous section to the Quantopian Research platform and used the
Page 7: Alphalens Python package for the analysis. Alphalens5 is a Python package for
Page 7: performance analysis of alpha factors, which can be used to create cross-sectional equity
Page 7: algorithms. Building a rigorous workflow with Alphalens enhances the robustness of
Page 7: backtesting strategies and reduces proneness to overfitting, both of which are important
Page 7: parameters in algorithm evaluation.
Page 7: For each signal, we conducted the Alphalens analysis, as follows: Eliminate all nulls; Map
Page 7: symbols to security objects in Quantopian; Index the dataframe properly, such that it is
Page 7: similar to pipeline output; Convert the dates to UTC; Obtain pricing data from the earliest
Page 7: date up to a month after the final date; Use the signal of t-1 and trade on the open date of
Page 7: t; Create the factor tear sheet using the signal and six periods (1, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 days).

Page 13: Conclusions and future work
Page 13: In this paper, we evaluated whether financial events extracted from digital media using an
Page 13: automatic information extraction platform constitute valuable trading signals. The effect
Page 13: of different events was determined in different time lags. We established that the polarity
Page 13: 13
Page 13:
Page 13: Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=2907600
Page 13:
Page 13:
Page 14: Event-Based Trading
Page 14:
Page 14: of different event-types is, indeed, reflected in the stock price in different time periods. We
Page 14: concluded that in contrast to aggregate polarity of events, which is typically used in
Page 14: sentiment analysis systems, event-types should be used as isolated signals. We further used
Page 14: a benchmark portfolio and demonstrated that superior returns can be obtained when
Page 14: considering different event-types for different investment periods. We found that both
Page 14: sentiments and events can generate positive alphas. We further found that Daily Sentiment
Page 14: Score signal is a better signal than Time-weighted Sentiment Score signal in the short term,
Page 14: and vice versa in the long term. When used aggregately, event-related information
Page 14: produces a noisy signal that does not improve the alpha of the Daily Sentiment Score signal.
Page 14: Using specific event-types, however, enhances the results of a strategy based on the Daily
Page 14: Sentiment Score signal. Event-types impact the Daily Sentiment Score signal differently
Page 14: in different time periods. We also compared the effect of the same event-type in different
Page 14: sectors, and in most cases, we found that the same event-types had a similar impact in all
Page 14: sectors. Consequently, we believe that sector-based information is less important in
Page 14: developing event-based trading strategies.
Page 14: Future work might further investigate how to use automated information extraction
Page 14: platforms to further enhance event-based trading strategies, or might focus on determining
Page 14: the effect of events in non-financial domains.
