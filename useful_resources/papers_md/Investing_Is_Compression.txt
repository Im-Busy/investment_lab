Page 1: Abstract. In 1956 John Kelly wrote a paper at Bell Labs describing the
Page 1: relationship between gambling and Information Theory. What came to be
Page 1: known as the Kelly Criterion is both an objective and a closed-form solution
Page 1: to sizing wagers when odds and edge are known. Samuelson argued it was
Page 1: arbitrary and subjective, and successfully kept it out of mainstream economics.
Page 1: Luckily it lived on in computer science, mostly because of Tom CoverΓÇÖs work
Page 1: at Stanford. He showed that it is the uniquely optimal way to invest: it
Page 1: maximizes long-term wealth, minimizes the risk of ruin, and is competitively
Page 1: optimal in a game-theoretic sense, even over the short term.
Page 1: One of CoverΓÇÖs most surprising contributions to portfolio theory was the
Page 1: universal portfolio. Related to universal compression in information theory, it
Page 1: performs asymptotically as well as the best constant-rebalanced portfolio in
Page 1: hindsight. I borrow a trick from that algorithm to show that KellyΓÇÖs objective,
Page 1: even in the general form, factors the investing problem into three terms: a
Page 1: money term, an entropy term, and a divergence term. The only way to maximize growth is to minimize divergence which measures the difference between
Page 1: our distribution and the true distribution in bits. Investing is, fundamentally,
Page 1: a compression problem.
Page 1: This decomposition also yields new practical results. Because the money
Page 1: and entropy terms are constant across strategies in a given backtest, the difference in log growth between two strategies measures their relative divergence
Page 1: in bits. I also introduce a winner fraction heuristic which allocates capital in
Page 1: proportion to each assetΓÇÖs probability of dominating the candidate set. The
Page 1: growth shortfall of this heuristic relative to the optimal portfolio is bounded
Page 1: by the entropy of the winner fraction distribution. To my knowledge, both the
Page 1: heuristic and the entropy bound are original contributions.

Page 1: 1. Introduction
Page 1: In 1956 John Kelly wrote a paper while working at Bell Labs [9] describing
Page 1: the relationship between gambling and Information Theory. The paper describes
Page 1: what would eventually be known as the Kelly criterion. The term Kelly criterion
Page 1: has come to signify two distinct things: the objective or utility function, as well
Page 1: as the closed-form solution to the problem when a gamblerΓÇÖs odds and edge are
Page 1: known. Kelly observed that simply maximizing expected wealth would surely lead
Page 1: to ruin given enough time. What was needed was some kind of nonlinear utility
Page 1: function to balance risk and reward. The math of utility functions came from Von
Page 1: Neumann and MorgensternΓÇÖs game theory. It formalizes a subjective preference or
Page 1: willingness to risk loss in pursuit of gain. Kelly wanted to avoid the ΓÇ£introduction of an arbitrary cost function,ΓÇ¥ likening it to the external value that people

Page 12: 5. Practical Applications
Page 12: 5.1. Backtesting. One of the challenges when backtesting and A/B testing of
Page 12: strategies is that market data is notoriously non-stationary. Even the log objective
Page 12: will be sensitive to extreme and atypical historical events. This makes the average
Page 12: difficult to interpret in a statistical sense when comparing strategies. But recall that
Page 12: the first two terms in equation 60 (money term and entropy term) do not depend
Page 12: on the allocation, so they are constant across strategies in a given backtest. The
Page 12: absolute value has no real statistical meaning, but the difference measures, in bits,
Page 12: the change in the divergence relative to some unknown ideal. This is reminiscent
Page 12: of Gibbs free energy in statistical mechanics.

Page 12: 5.2. MDL, Market Prediction, and Strategy Performance. Markets are
Page 12: high-dimensional, sparse, and non-stationary. This makes it very difficult to separate signal from noise. It is especially challenging for techniques based on statistical
Page 12: learning theory [20] which rely on resampling from a stationary stochastic source
Page 12: to detect overfitting and generalization. Although statistical learning theory is the
Page 12: dominant machine learning paradigm, itΓÇÖs not the only one. Information theory
Page 12: offers alternative learning paradigms, including the MDL principle [8, 10]; see [16]
Page 12: for a discussion of prediction, compression, and the relationship between models
Page 12: and data. The key insight is that randomness is incompressible, so generalization
Page 12: is about finding a parsimonious, compressive description. The relationship between
Page 12: strategy performance and prediction performance is more direct when both are
Page 12: treated as information theoretic questions. In other words, itΓÇÖs easier to draw a
Page 12: clear line between signal improvements and strategy improvements when both are
Page 12: measured in bits.

Page 14: 6. Conclusion
Page 14: The Kelly criterion describes how to allocate capital in simple games of chance
Page 14: with known odds and edge. It is a closed form optimal solution to KellyΓÇÖs log
Page 14: wealth objective. But it turns out that KellyΓÇÖs objective is itself optimal among all
Page 14: objectives. And this fact reframes the investing problem itself.
Page 14: In finance, we often think of risk and reward as two different axes: for a given
Page 14: amount of risk, we maximize return. This goes back to the 1940ΓÇÖs and MarkowitzΓÇÖs
Page 14: Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) [12]. A concern with MPT is that markets are
Page 14: heavy-tail and so diversification (averaging) may not reduce risk (variance) [17].
Page 14: This is fundamentally a statistical concern. But even if markets were Gaussian, it
Page 14: would still be suboptimal because KellyΓÇÖs objective is uniquely optimal. To justify
Page 14: that, we have to turn from statistics to information theory.
Page 14: Instead of thinking of risk and reward as two different axes, Kelly proposed
Page 14: maximizing a single objective, the logarithm of wealth over uncertainty. In other
Page 14: words the expectation or the probability weighted average log return. Samuelson
Page 14: argued against this idea, successfully keeping it out of mainstream economics.
Page 14: We now know because of Tom CoverΓÇÖs work at Stanford that this was not a
Page 14: subjective, arbitrary utility function as Samuelson believed. It is the objectively
Page 14: optimal way to invest. It is competitively optimal in a game-theoretic sense, assuming neither party has an information advantage. It dominates over almost every
Page 14: market sequence. Even over the short term.
Page 14: With the help of a transformation from CoverΓÇÖs universal portfolio algorithm, we
Page 14: now understand what is so powerful about KellyΓÇÖs objective. It reveals the information structure of the investing problem, factoring it into three terms: a money term
Page 14: (payoff / rules of the game), an entropy term (uncertainty) and a divergence term.
Page 14: The only way to maximize compounding growth rate is to minimize the friction
Page 14: from that divergence term. Fundamentally, investing is a compression problem.
